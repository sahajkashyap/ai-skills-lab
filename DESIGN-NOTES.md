# Design notes — what changed and why

*This is the revision record for Module 1 of AI Skills for Everyone. The
module began in May 2026 as a 20-slide deck for teaching live. In August 2026
it was rebuilt as two connected artifacts: the facilitator deck and a
self-paced hands-on lab. Every change below was a deliberate teaching
decision, recorded the way I record curriculum changes: what changed, then
why. The habit comes from classroom practice — when a lesson gets revised,
the reason gets written down, or the next revision undoes it.*

---

## The big structural change: from watch-me to do-it-yourself

**What changed:** The deck's "TRY ASKING" prompts became six full exercises in
a lab, each with the same four beats: Try it → What you should see → Push it
further → Check yourself.

**Why:** A slide tells a learner what AI can do. An exercise has them do it
with their own email, their own task, their own expertise, and then check the
result themselves. Adults learn tools by using them on real work, and every
exercise in the lab requires the learner's real work. There are no practice
examples on purpose.

## Measuring learning instead of completion

**What changed:** The lab now opens with a one-question confidence rating
(can you catch AI being wrong?), captures the hallucination each learner
catches in Exercise 6 in their own words, and closes with a short scored
assessment: three multiple-choice questions, a free-response, and the
prompt-specificity move performed one more time. The learner sees a score,
a before-and-after confidence comparison, and can download their results.

**Why:** Finishing a page proves scrolling, not learning. The assessment
checks the two things the lab actually teaches: did you personally catch a
confident wrong answer, and can you make a vague request specific. Both are
observable. Neither can be faked by skimming.

## "Computer helper" became "a digital companion, a partner you can work with"

**What changed:** Slide 2's definition of AI.

**Why:** "Helper" frames AI as an appliance. The whole module argues for a
working partnership where the human keeps the final say, so the definition
now plants that frame in the first minute.

## Credit before caution

**What changed:** Slide 4 now says AI "reads context, and it can usually
figure out, pretty accurately, what you are trying to say" *before* it says
"underneath, it is guessing the next word."

**Why:** Adults have used these tools. A lesson that opens by underselling
them loses the room. Give the capability its honest due first, then the
caution lands as calibration rather than fear.

## Every skill got a two-level prompt

**What changed:** Slides 6, 7, 10, and 13 now show a simple ask and then a
more specific ask (a work party, then a work party where everyone can join;
a plan, then a plan with the details only you know; a fact-check, then a
fact-check that demands the exact source sentence).

**Why:** The single most useful prompt skill is specificity, and it is
better demonstrated than described. Showing the same request twice, once
plain and once specific, teaches the move without a single line of theory.
The final assessment asks the learner to perform exactly this move.

## The hallucination section was rebuilt around stakes

**What changed:** The old slide said "AI can make stuff up" and defined
hallucination. The new sequence says AI is exactly as confident when it is
wrong as when it is right, and then gives a rule: match your double-checking
to the stakes. A ball score needs nothing. A financial analysis gets sources,
links, and a second check. A new diagram shows a lead AI dispatching three
smaller checker-AIs: the sources, the numbers, the facts.

**Why:** "It can be wrong" without a rule produces either paranoia or
shrugging. A stakes rule is a durable habit that survives model
improvements. And "ask for a confidence percentage" was cut from an early
draft because a language model's stated percentage is itself generated text —
the honest ask is for the exact sentence from the source.

## The Tin Man became the Silicon Man

**What changed:** The "can't feel" slide is now titled The Silicon Man, with
a custom illustration: a golden metal man with a microchip where the heart
would go, standing beside a person whose heart glows.

**Why:** Adults remember characters better than category labels. The name is
original, and it sets up the slide's closing line: when something matters
deeply, AI can help you write, but your words are more than silicon.

## "Not always right" became "You're Responsible"

**What changed:** The final watch-for slide reframed from a fact about AI to
a fact about the person using it: when a mistake goes out, the one who signed
off owns it, and with AI the one who signs off is you. When in doubt: read
your own work, and ask a fresh AI that has never seen the work to fact-check
it and report back.

**Why:** Responsibility is the durable mindset underneath every verification
habit. And the fresh-reviewer instruction is a principle I build into my own
tools: a checker that has seen the work made is grading its own homework.

## The close became a checklist

**What changed:** The last slide's "AI gives an answer, you check it" now
shows three ticked boxes: AI gives an answer, a second AI checks it, you
read it yourself.

**Why:** A checklist is the visual form of a habit, and habits are what this
module is for. A late revision changed the order's framing: the checklist now
begins with "you set the task," because the earlier "AI goes first" phrasing
undersold the truth that the human's input shapes everything downstream. The
partnership reads: you direct, AI produces, an agent you direct checks, you
read it last. A ladder cannot decide which wall to lean on.

## Register: warmth kept, phonics cut

**What changed:** The pronunciation respelling of "hallucination" was
removed; the word card and plain definition stay. Same for a few phrasings
that carried an elementary-classroom register into an adult room.

**Why:** The plain language is the product. But plain and childlike are
different registers, and an adult audience deserves the first without the
second.

## The illustrations

**What changed:** The original image set was AI-generated to a consistent
style. The August revision added four purpose-drawn pieces in the same
palette: the four-panel hand-off comic (AI reads, hands it to a person, the
person reads, the final okay), the checker-team diagram, the Silicon Man,
and the vending-machine-versus-thinking-partner pair.

A later pass split the hand-off comic the way a picture book demands: its
first two panels (read it all, get the short version) stay with the
summarizing slide, and the full five-panel loop moved to the closing
checklist slide it illustrates, so the pictures land where the words do.

**Why:** Generated art is part of the production pipeline and it is good at
scenes. But when a slide's idea is a system — a hand-off, a dispatch, an
absence where a heart should be — a drawn diagram states the idea more
precisely than a generated scene can. The mix is deliberate: generate where
a scene will do, draw where the idea is the picture.

## One taxonomy across both artifacts

**What changed:** The deck and the lab were reconciled to teach the same
named ideas in the same order: it can make stuff up (and how to make it show
its work), it doesn't know you (vending machine versus thinking partner),
the Silicon Man, and you're responsible.

**Why:** The deck is the live format and the lab is the self-paced format of
the same curriculum. If the formats drift, a learner who touches both gets
two vocabularies for one idea, and the ideas stop sticking.

## Independent review before publishing

**What changed:** Before publication, the whole artifact went through an
independent review pass by a reviewer who had no hand in writing it, briefed
to read it skeptically. The dead-link, register, taxonomy, and measurement
findings above came out of that review and were fixed before anything went
live.

**Why:** Same principle as the fresh-AI fact-check the module teaches. The
person who wrote a thing is the worst-placed person to catch what is wrong
with it. That rule applies to me too.
